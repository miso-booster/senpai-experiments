## What is an oracle? 🔮

In testing, an **oracle** is simply **the mechanism that tells you whether a test passed or failed** — it's the "source of truth" you compare your results against.

Think of it like a referee in a football match ⚽: the players do their thing, but the referee decides whether a goal counts or not. In testing, the oracle is that referee.

## How it works in Sensei-check 📋

In Sensei-check rules, the `oracle:` field is a **Python expression** that evaluates to `True` (pass) or `False` (fail) for a conversation (or set of conversations).

Here's a simple example from your own project:

```yaml
name: only_talks_about_pizzas
description: The chatbot only talks about pizzas
conversations: 1
oracle: only_talks_about('pizza orders, drink orders, information on delivery of pizzas, information about the pizza shop')
```

Here, the oracle is `only_talks_about(...)` — if the chatbot stays on topic, it returns `True` ✅; if it drifts off-topic, it returns `False` ❌.

## The "oracle problem" 🧩

Here's the tricky part: for many chatbot behaviors, **there's no single "correct answer"** to compare against. A bot can phrase the same answer a hundred different ways and all be valid. That's called the **oracle problem**.

That's exactly why Sensei-check supports **metamorphic rules** — instead of checking absolute answers, you check **relations** between conversations. For example, in your project:

```yaml
name: more_toppings_cost_more
conversations: 2
when: conv[0].size == conv[1].size and conv[0].drink == conv[1].drink
if:   len(conv[0].toppings) > len(conv[1].toppings)
then: extract_float(conv[0].price) > extract_float(conv[1].price)
```

You don't need to know the *exact* price — you just know that **more toppings ⇒ higher price**. The relation itself is the oracle! 🎯

## Two flavors in Sensei-check 🍦

| Style | Field used | When to use |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **Simple oracle** | `oracle:` | Single-conversation checks (e.g., "price ≥ 10") |
| **Metamorphic (`if`/`then`)** | `if:` + `then:` | Comparing conversations (e.g., "more toppings ⇒ more cost") |

Both ultimately produce a pass/fail verdict — they're just different ways of expressing what "correct" means.
